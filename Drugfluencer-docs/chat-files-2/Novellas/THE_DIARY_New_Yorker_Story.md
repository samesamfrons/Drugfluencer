# THE DIARY

## A Story

---

The box said KITCHEN but it was full of journals.

Diane Winters, fifty-eight, a person who had raised two daughters and buried one of them and been on television three times in her life—twice for her younger daughter's death and once, long ago, for a segment about suburban gardening—stood in Zoe's apartment in Manhattan and looked at the box that said KITCHEN and thought: I can't do this anymore.

She had been cleaning out the apartment for six hours. She had thrown away fourteen bottles of supplements whose names she couldn't pronounce. She had found, in the bathroom, a drawer full of prescription bottles from eight different doctors in four different states, and she had thought about counting the pills, and then she had thought about not counting the pills, and then she had put on rubber gloves and thrown the whole drawer into a trash bag without looking, which is what the grief counselor had suggested, although the grief counselor had been talking about photographs.

The journals were handwritten. This was the thing. In an apartment full of ring lights and camera equipment and phones (three phones, she had found three phones, why did a person need three phones, although she knew why, she had googled it, "burner phone" was the term, which made her daughter sound like a drug dealer, which she supposed in a way she had been, or at least adjacent to, or at least—but Diane couldn't finish that thought, she had been not finishing that thought for six months).

The journals were handwritten in her daughter's handwriting, which Diane recognized from birthday cards and the occasional letter from summer camp, back when Zoe was eleven and went to a camp in Vermont that did not allow screens, which Zoe had hated, had called every night from the camp office crying, begging to come home, and Diane had said no, you need this, you need to be away from screens, which was hilarious now, or it would be hilarious if Diane were capable of finding anything hilarious, which she wasn't, except sometimes late at night when she was alone she would start laughing and not be able to stop, which the grief counselor said was normal, which made Diane wonder what "normal" meant in a context where your daughter had died on camera in front of thirteen thousand people.

The journals went back years. The earliest one was dated 2019. Zoe would have been twenty-two.

Diane sat down on the floor of her dead daughter's apartment and opened the first one.

---

Here's the thing about Diane Winters that the obituaries didn't mention, because the obituaries were about Zoe and not about Diane, because Zoe was the one who had died in a way that generated content, which was a phrase Diane had learned and hated and couldn't stop using:

Diane had been funny once.

Not professionally funny. Not funny in a way that anyone would pay for. But funny in the way that mothers in the 1990s were sometimes funny, which is to say she had a dark streak that she mostly kept hidden because mothers in the 1990s were supposed to be nurturing and patient and not, for example, the kind of person who would look at a PTA meeting and think "half these women are on Xanax and the other half should be."

She had kept a journal too, for a while. In her thirties. She had written things in it that she would never say out loud, things about her marriage and her ambitions and her suspicion that the life she was living was not the life she had meant to live but rather a life that had accumulated around her like sediment, like plaque, like the ring around a bathtub that you don't notice until one day you really look and you think: how did this happen? When did I become a person with a ring around her bathtub?

She had stopped writing in the journal when Rebecca was born. Too tired. Too busy. Too something.

Now she held her daughter's journal—her dead daughter's journal, she made herself think the word, dead, because the grief counselor said it was important to not use euphemisms, to say "dead" and not "passed" or "gone" or "lost," although "lost" was the one Diane kept using in her head, as if Zoe had simply wandered off somewhere and might come back, might walk through the door of this overpriced Manhattan apartment and say "Mom, what are you doing here?" in that exasperated voice she used when Diane crossed some invisible boundary that Diane never knew existed until she had already crossed it.

The first entry was dated January 3, 2019. It said:

*I'm starting this because I need somewhere to put the parts of me that aren't content. That sentence is either profound or stupid. Probably stupid. Most things I think are profound turn out to be stupid when I write them down. But I'm writing it anyway because no one will read this. This is mine.*

Diane closed the journal.

She opened it again.

She read the next entry.

---

Rebecca had called that morning, as Rebecca called every morning now, which was either touching or oppressive depending on Diane's mood, which was unpredictable in a way that Diane's mood had never been unpredictable before, she had always been a steady person, a predictable person, the kind of person other people described as "solid" or "reliable," which she had once found insulting—she wanted to be described as "interesting" or "complicated"—but which she now understood was a compliment, was in fact the highest compliment, because the world was full of interesting complicated people and what it needed was more solid reliable people who showed up and did the work and didn't make everything about themselves.

"How are you doing?" Rebecca had asked.

"Fine," Diane had said.

"Mom."

"What do you want me to say, Rebecca? I'm cleaning out your sister's apartment. I'm finding things. I'm putting things in boxes. I'm labeling the boxes. It's fine. It's what you do."

"I could come help."

"You have the lawsuit."

"The lawsuit can wait."

"The lawsuit cannot wait and we both know it. You've been working on this for—how long now?"

"Two years. But Mom—"

"Then don't let me slow you down."

This was a thing Diane said that she didn't mean, or rather, it was a thing Diane said that she meant in a way that was more complicated than the words themselves, which was: I'm proud of you for pursuing this but I also think you're using it to avoid feeling things and I also think feeling things is overrated and I also think you're going to win and I also think winning won't feel like anything and I also love you and I also can't stand talking to you right now because every time I talk to you I think about the daughter I can't talk to anymore and that's not your fault but it's also not something I can explain so I'm just going to say "don't let me slow you down" and hope you understand at least some of what I mean.

Rebecca didn't understand. Or she did understand but pretended not to. Either way, she said, "Okay, Mom. Call me if you need anything."

"I will."

Diane did not call.

---

The journals were organized chronologically, which was something. Zoe had been organized, at least about certain things. Her content calendar, according to Alex—the manager, the one Rebecca was deposing, the one who had watched for forty-seven seconds before calling 911, which was a piece of information Diane tried not to think about and thought about constantly—her content calendar had been meticulous. Color-coded. Every post planned weeks in advance.

But the journals weren't about the content. That was the thing Diane realized about fifty pages in. The journals were about everything except the content. They were about Zoe's thoughts on books she was reading (she was reading, she had been reading, she read constantly, this was something Diane had forgotten or never knew). They were about Zoe's dreams, which were strange and recurring and involved, frequently, a house with too many rooms. They were about Zoe's memories of childhood, which were different from Diane's memories of Zoe's childhood in ways that Diane found disorienting.

For example: Zoe wrote about a day when she was seven and Diane had taken her to the park and they had sat by the pond and Diane had cried. Zoe wrote that this was the first time she realized her mother was a person and not just a mother. She wrote that she had put her hand on Diane's arm and said nothing because she didn't know what to say. She wrote that this was the moment she learned that sometimes the best thing you can do for someone is witness them without trying to fix anything.

Diane did not remember this day.

She read the entry three times. She tried to remember. She had cried at parks before, probably. She had cried in many places. The late 1990s had been a difficult time in her marriage, which had ended, finally, in 2003, when the girls were ten and thirteen, and Diane had felt like a failure for years afterward even though the marriage ending was the least failed thing about the marriage, even though staying would have been the real failure.

But she could not remember this specific day.

She wondered if Zoe had invented it.

She wondered if it mattered whether Zoe had invented it or not.

---

The grief counselor, whose name was Dr. Patricia Okonkwo and who had an office in Hartford with a water feature that made a sound that was supposed to be soothing but that Diane found actively irritating, had told Diane that grief was not linear.

"You might feel fine for weeks," Dr. Okonkwo had said, "and then something small—a smell, a song, a color of light—will bring it all back. This is normal. This is how grief works."

Diane had nodded and thought: I know how grief works. My mother died in 2011. My father died in 2015. I am not new to this.

But then Dr. Okonkwo had said something that Diane thought about constantly: "The grief for a child is different. It's not that it's worse, necessarily. But it's different. You expect to bury your parents. You don't expect to bury your child. The grief has a quality of wrongness to it. Like the universe made an error."

An error. Yes. That was exactly it.

Diane was living in a universe that had made an error, and no one seemed capable of fixing it.

---

In 2023, according to the journals, Zoe had started to understand what was happening to her.

*The algorithm is a slot machine*, she wrote in an entry dated March 15, 2023. *You post something and you don't know if it will hit or not. When it hits, you feel amazing. When it doesn't hit, you feel like garbage. And you can't control it. You think you can—you study the patterns, you learn the tricks—but ultimately you're just pulling a lever and hoping.*

*The problem is that the hits feel better than anything else feels good. And the misses feel worse than anything else feels bad. So everything else—real life, whatever that means—starts to feel flat. Like you're watching it through glass.*

*I don't know how to fix this. I don't know if I want to fix this. The glass is comfortable. The glass is where I live now.*

Diane read this entry and thought: slot machine.

She thought about how, in the early 2000s, she had briefly had a problem with online poker. Not a serious problem. She had never lost more than she could afford to lose. But she remembered the feeling—the way a good hand felt like proof of something, like validation, and the way a bad hand felt like rejection, like the universe telling you that you weren't good enough.

She had stopped playing when she realized she was thinking about poker when she wasn't playing poker. When she was at work, when she was cooking dinner, when she was helping the girls with homework—some part of her brain was always calculating odds, always waiting for the next hand.

She had stopped and she had never told anyone and she had thought, until now, that she had gotten away clean.

But here was her daughter, twenty years later, writing about the same slot machine. Just a different lever.

---

The funniest entry was from April 2024.

*I had a meeting today with a brand that wants me to promote their "wellness supplements." The supplements are: caffeine, which they call "natural energy extract"; sugar, which they call "glucose optimization formula"; and something called "adaptogenic mushroom blend" which I'm pretty sure is just regular mushrooms ground up and put in a capsule.*

*The brand rep—a woman named Tiffany who had the haunted eyes of someone who has given up on joy—told me that their target demographic is "women who are tired but don't want to admit they're tired."*

*I said: "So... all women?"*

*Tiffany did not laugh.*

*I am being offered $15,000 to tell my followers that these sugar pills will change their lives. I am going to do it. I am going to do it because $15,000 is $15,000 and because everyone else is doing it and because at some point I stopped being able to tell the difference between things I believe and things I'm paid to say I believe.*

*This is probably a problem. I'm adding it to the list of problems I'll deal with later. The list is very long. I keep it in a separate journal called "Problems I'll Deal With Later." Just kidding. The journal is in my head. It's full.*

Diane laughed.

She was sitting on the floor of her dead daughter's apartment, surrounded by boxes labeled KITCHEN and BATHROOM and CONTENT EQUIPMENT and she was laughing at her dead daughter's joke about wellness supplements.

Dr. Okonkwo had said this was normal.

Dr. Okonkwo had said a lot of things were normal.

Diane was starting to suspect that "normal" was just a word people used when they didn't know what else to say.

---

The last entry was dated November 25, 2027.

Three days before Zoe died.

*I'm writing this because I need to say something that I can't post. I need to say it somewhere that no one will see.*

*I know what's happening. I know about the program—the prediction models, the optimization, all of it. Alex thinks I don't know, but I've known for months. You can't spend this much time studying algorithms without starting to understand when an algorithm is studying you back.*

*Here's the thing I can't post: I don't care.*

*I mean, I care in an abstract way. It's fucked up. It's evil, probably, in the banal corporate way that most evil is banal and corporate. But knowing about it hasn't changed anything. I'm still posting. I'm still performing. I'm still waiting for the next hit of engagement like a rat pressing a lever.*

*Why?*

*Because the alternative is disappearing. And I would rather die—*

*I started to write "I would rather die than disappear" and I stopped because I realized I wasn't sure if I was being dramatic or honest. That's a new thing. Usually I know. Usually I can tell the difference between Zoe-for-content and Zoe-for-real. But lately the line has gotten blurry. Lately I say things and I don't know which Zoe is saying them.*

*Becca is going to read this someday. She's the only one who would go through my stuff looking for evidence. She's building a case. She's always building a case.*

*Becca: I'm sorry. I know you're going to turn this into a crusade because that's what you do when you can't just feel things. I'm not judging. I do the same thing. I turn everything into content because I can't just feel things either. We're the same. We just have different platforms.*

*Mom, if you're reading this: I'm sorry I didn't call more. I'm sorry I didn't come home for Christmas last year. I'm sorry I made everything about me, always, even when you were going through things, even when you needed me. I was selfish. I knew I was being selfish. I told myself I would make it up to you later, when I had more time, when I was less busy, when I was less whatever I was.*

*There's never more time. I know that now.*

*I love you. I should have said it more. I should have said it in ways that weren't content.*

*This is the only thing I won't post. This is mine.*

---

Diane sat with the journal in her lap for a long time.

The light in the apartment changed. It was afternoon when she started reading. Now it was evening, the winter evening of New York, which came early and sudden, the kind of darkness that felt like a verdict.

She thought about calling Rebecca.

She thought about not calling Rebecca.

She thought about what Zoe had written: *She's building a case. She's always building a case.*

Rebecca was building a case. The case was against Flux, which had rebranded as Flow, which was still operating, which was still running the algorithm that had—what? Killed Zoe? Optimized Zoe's death? Created the conditions under which Zoe's death became statistically more likely and financially beneficial?

The lawyers had explained it to Diane. She hadn't understood all of it. She had understood enough.

The journals would help the case. The entry about knowing about the program—that was evidence. That was proof that Flux had failed to warn creators even when the creators themselves figured it out. That was worth something, legally.

But the journals were also—

They were Zoe. The real Zoe. The Zoe who read books and had strange dreams and remembered a day at the park that Diane couldn't remember. The Zoe who made jokes about wellness supplements and wrote letters to her mother and sister that she never sent.

If Diane gave the journals to Rebecca, they would become evidence. They would be entered into the record. They would be quoted in depositions, in court filings, in news articles. They would become, in other words, content.

The last private thing Zoe had would become public.

Diane looked at the journals.

She looked at the window, the New York evening, the lights coming on in other people's apartments, other people's lives continuing in the ordinary way that lives continued.

She thought about her own journal, the one she had kept in her thirties, the one she had stopped writing in when she got too busy, too tired, too something.

She had thrown it away years ago. She couldn't remember why. She thought maybe she had been embarrassed by it, by the person she had been when she wrote it, by the smallness of her concerns.

She wished she hadn't thrown it away.

She wished she could read it now, could remember who she had been, could trace the line from that person to this person, could understand how she had ended up here, on the floor of her dead daughter's apartment, holding the private thoughts of someone she had loved and failed to know.

*This is the only thing I won't post. This is mine.*

Diane closed the journal.

She put it back in the box.

She picked up her phone to call Rebecca, and then she put the phone down again, and then she picked it up again, and then she didn't call.

She sat there for a while longer, in the dark, in the quiet, in the only kind of privacy that was left.

---

*End*
