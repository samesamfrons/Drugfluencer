# THE DIARY

## A Story

---

The box said KITCHEN but it was full of journals.

Diane Winters, fifty-eight, a person who had raised two daughters and buried one of them and been on television three times in her life—twice for her younger daughter's death and once, long ago, for a segment about suburban gardening that had aired on a local Connecticut station at 6 AM on a Saturday, which meant approximately eleven people had seen it, one of whom was her mother, who had called afterward to say "you looked tired, honey, are you sleeping enough?"—stood in Zoe's apartment in Manhattan and looked at the box that said KITCHEN and thought: I can't do this anymore.

She had been cleaning out the apartment for six hours. She had thrown away fourteen bottles of supplements whose names she couldn't pronounce. She had found, in the bathroom, a drawer full of prescription bottles from eight different doctors in four different states, and she had thought about counting the pills, and then she had thought about not counting the pills, and then she had put on rubber gloves and thrown the whole drawer into a trash bag without looking, which is what the grief counselor had suggested, although the grief counselor had been talking about photographs, not pills, and Diane wasn't sure the advice transferred.

The grief counselor's name was Dr. Patricia Okonkwo and she had an office in Hartford with a water feature that made a sound that was supposed to be soothing but that Diane found actively irritating, like someone had recorded a toilet that wouldn't stop running and was playing it on a loop to see how long before Diane cracked and said something. She hadn't said anything. She had sat in Dr. Okonkwo's tasteful beige office with its tasteful beige furniture and its diplomas on the wall (Yale, then Columbia, then a certification in "trauma-informed grief therapy" from an institution Diane had never heard of) and she had nodded and said "mm-hmm" and "yes, I see" and "that makes sense" while thinking about the water feature and whether Dr. Okonkwo knew it was annoying or whether this was some kind of test.

The journals were handwritten. This was the thing. In an apartment full of ring lights and camera equipment and phones (three phones, she had found three phones, why did a person need three phones, although she knew why, she had googled it, "burner phone" was the term, which made her daughter sound like a drug dealer, which she supposed in a way she had been, or at least adjacent to, or at least—but Diane couldn't finish that thought, she had been not-finishing that thought for six months, the thought just ended in an ellipsis and then her brain moved on to something else, like how the light in the apartment was too bright, or how the kitchen didn't have a real stove, just a two-burner thing that looked like it had never been used).

The journals were handwritten in her daughter's handwriting, which Diane recognized from birthday cards and the occasional letter from summer camp, back when Zoe was eleven and went to a camp in Vermont that did not allow screens, which Zoe had hated, had called every night from the camp office crying, begging to come home, and Diane had said no, you need this, you need to be away from screens, which was hilarious now, or it would be hilarious if Diane were capable of finding anything hilarious, which she wasn't, except sometimes late at night when she was alone she would start laughing and not be able to stop, which the grief counselor said was normal, which made Diane wonder what "normal" meant in a context where your daughter had died on camera in front of thirteen thousand people while her manager—a person named Alex who wore expensive clothes and had a face like a closed door—watched for forty-seven seconds before calling 911.

Forty-seven seconds. Diane had done the math. She had timed herself, once, sitting in her kitchen in Connecticut, staring at the clock on the microwave, watching forty-seven seconds pass. It was a long time. It was long enough to walk to the bathroom and back. It was long enough to make a decision. It was long enough to do something, anything, and Alex had done nothing, had watched, had—according to the documents Rebecca had obtained—watched the engagement metrics climb while Zoe's eyes closed.

The journals went back years. The earliest one was dated 2019. Zoe would have been twenty-two.

Diane sat down on the floor of her dead daughter's apartment—the rent was $4,200 a month, which was obscene, which was more than Diane's mortgage payment, which Zoe had paid in cash, always in cash, because influencers, Diane had learned, were paid in ways that made everything complicated, 1099s and brand deals and "gifted" items that had to be declared as income, a whole economy that existed alongside the regular economy but with different rules, rules that Diane didn't understand and didn't want to understand—and opened the first journal.

---

Here's the thing about Diane Winters that the obituaries didn't mention, because the obituaries were about Zoe and not about Diane, because Zoe was the one who had died in a way that generated content, which was a phrase Diane had learned and hated and couldn't stop using, it had colonized her vocabulary the way so many of Zoe's phrases had colonized her vocabulary, "content" and "engagement" and "algorithm" and "platform," words that Diane had once associated with bridges and voting and math were now associated with her daughter's death:

Diane had been funny once.

Not professionally funny. Not funny in a way that anyone would pay for. But funny in the way that mothers in the 1990s were sometimes funny, which is to say she had a dark streak that she mostly kept hidden because mothers in the 1990s were supposed to be nurturing and patient and soft-voiced and endlessly available, were supposed to be, basically, furniture that could also make sandwiches, and Diane had been that, had performed that, but underneath she was the kind of person who would look at a PTA meeting and think "half these women are on Xanax and the other half should be," the kind of person who had once described her husband's mother as "a woman who has never had an unexpressed thought in her life," the kind of person who kept her real opinions in a locked box somewhere behind her sternum and only let them out late at night, when the kids were asleep and the house was quiet and she could sit on the back porch with a glass of wine and think about all the things she would say if she were a different kind of person living a different kind of life.

She had kept a journal too, for a while. In her thirties. She had written things in it that she would never say out loud, things about her marriage (which was not good, which had never been good, which had been tolerable and then intolerable and then finally, mercifully, over) and her ambitions (which were vague but persistent, a sense that she was supposed to be doing something, though she couldn't say what) and her suspicion that the life she was living was not the life she had meant to live but rather a life that had accumulated around her like sediment, like plaque, like the ring around a bathtub that you don't notice until one day you really look and you think: how did this happen? When did I become a person with a ring around her bathtub?

She had stopped writing in the journal when Rebecca was born. Too tired. Too busy. Too something. The journal had sat in a drawer for years, and then one day, during the divorce, when Diane was packing up the house and separating her things from her husband's things and trying to figure out which things were really hers and which things were just things she had lived with for so long that she had forgotten they weren't hers, she had found the journal and opened it and read a few entries and felt a wave of embarrassment so strong it was almost physical, a hot flush that started in her chest and spread to her face, and she had thrown the journal away without reading any more of it.

She regretted that now.

She regretted a lot of things now, but she regretted that specifically, because if she had the journal she could read it and remember who she had been, could trace the line from that person to this person, from the thirty-two-year-old who wrote about her vague ambitions to the fifty-eight-year-old who was sitting on the floor of her dead daughter's apartment surrounded by boxes labeled KITCHEN and BATHROOM and CONTENT EQUIPMENT.

(Content equipment. That's what Rebecca had called it. "The content equipment goes in its own box, Mom, don't mix it with the personal stuff." As if there was a difference. As if any of it was personal, at this point.)

Now she held her daughter's journal—her dead daughter's journal, she made herself think the word, dead, because the grief counselor said it was important to not use euphemisms, to say "dead" and not "passed" or "gone" or "lost," although "lost" was the one Diane kept using in her head, as if Zoe had simply wandered off somewhere and might come back, might walk through the door of this overpriced Manhattan apartment and say "Mom, what are you doing here?" in that exasperated voice she used when Diane crossed some invisible boundary that Diane never knew existed until she had already crossed it—and she thought about how strange it was that both of them, mother and daughter, had kept journals, had needed a place to put the things they couldn't say out loud, and neither of them had ever mentioned it to the other.

What else hadn't they mentioned?

What else was in these pages?

The first entry was dated January 3, 2019. It said:

*I'm starting this because I need somewhere to put the parts of me that aren't content. That sentence is either profound or stupid. Probably stupid. Most things I think are profound turn out to be stupid when I write them down. But I'm writing it anyway because no one will read this. This is mine.*

*New Year's resolution: figure out who I am when no one's watching. Realize as I write this that I don't know. Realize as I write THAT that this is maybe a problem.*

*Steps to figure out who I am when no one's watching:*
*1. Stop watching myself*
*2. ???*
*3. Profit (joke) (sort of)*

*The algorithm knows me better than I know myself. That's not a metaphor. It's literally true. It knows what I'll click on, what I'll watch, what I'll share. It knows my patterns. It knows my weaknesses. It knows that I'll watch videos about organizing closets even though I've never organized a closet in my life. It knows that I'll click on articles about celebrities I don't care about. It knows that I'll engage with content that makes me angry because anger is engaging.*

*If the algorithm knows all that, and I don't—I mean, I didn't know I cared about closet organization until the algorithm showed me that I cared about closet organization—then who's the real me? The me who thinks she doesn't care about closets, or the me who watches closet videos at 2 AM?*

*This is either a profound question or a stupid question. Probably stupid. But I'm writing it anyway.*

Diane closed the journal.

She looked around the apartment. It was getting dark. She had been here for—she checked her phone—seven hours. She should eat something. She should call Rebecca. She should do any number of things that normal people did when they were cleaning out their dead daughter's apartment on a Tuesday in January.

She opened the journal again.

---

The second entry was dated January 7, 2019. Four days later.

*Okay so I didn't write for four days because I was "busy" which is a lie, I wasn't busy, I was avoiding this, because writing in here means confronting the thing I said about not knowing who I am when no one's watching, and I don't want to confront that, I want to scroll through my phone and not think about it.*

*But I'm writing now because something happened.*

*I was at a coffee shop—the one on Bedford, the one with the good oat milk lattes and the bad WiFi, which I usually avoid because bad WiFi is death, but I went anyway because I was meeting this guy, this guy I matched with on Hinge, and he wanted to meet there, and I said yes because I'm trying to say yes to things, trying to be spontaneous, trying to be the kind of person who goes to coffee shops with bad WiFi.*

*The guy was fine. Average. He worked in "finance" which could mean anything from investment banking to being a teller at a credit union, I didn't ask, I didn't care, I was already composing the tweet about the date in my head, already thinking about how I would describe him to my followers ("finance bro, beige personality, ordered an Americano like that tells you something about a person, which maybe it does").*

*And then he said something. He said: "You're not really here, are you?"*

*I said: "What do you mean?"*

*He said: "You're thinking about something else. You're thinking about how you're going to describe this later."*

*And I was. I was doing exactly that.*

*He wasn't mad about it. He wasn't accusing me of anything. He just... noticed. And then he said: "It's okay. I do it too. Everyone does it now. We're all narrating our lives for an audience that isn't here."*

*I didn't know what to say. I still don't know what to say. But I've been thinking about it for four days. An audience that isn't here. That's what I have in my head, all the time. An audience that isn't here but might be, at any moment, if I post something.*

*The guy and I didn't go on a second date. I didn't even make the tweet.*

*Some things, I'm learning, are not content.*

*This is one of them. This is mine.*

---

Diane read this entry twice. She thought about the guy at the coffee shop, this person her daughter had met once and never seen again, who had said something that had stuck. She thought about how strange it was that a stranger could see something that Diane, who had known Zoe for twenty-seven years, had never seen.

Or had she seen it?

Had she seen it and not said anything, because saying something would have meant acknowledging that her daughter was not okay, that her daughter's relationship to the world had become mediated by something Diane didn't understand, that her daughter was living a life Diane couldn't access?

She had seen it. She had seen it and done nothing, because doing something would have been—what? An intrusion. A boundary violation. A failure to respect Zoe's autonomy, her adulthood, her right to make her own choices.

These were the things Diane had told herself. These were the things she had told the grief counselor, who had nodded and said "mm-hmm" and "that's understandable" and "you were respecting her boundaries."

But now, reading the journal, Diane thought: what if the boundaries were the problem? What if respecting Zoe's boundaries had just been another way of saying "I don't want to deal with this"? What if every time Diane had decided not to say something, not to ask a question, not to push a little harder, she had been making a choice—a choice to protect herself from the discomfort of confrontation, disguised as a choice to protect Zoe's autonomy?

She didn't know. She would never know. That was the thing about death—it ended all the conversations you hadn't had yet, turned all the things you meant to say into things you would never say, transformed every small silence into a silence that would last forever.

---

Rebecca called at 6 PM, as Rebecca called every evening now, which was either touching or oppressive depending on Diane's mood, which was unpredictable in a way that Diane's mood had never been unpredictable before.

"How are you doing?" Rebecca asked.

"I'm reading her journals," Diane said. She hadn't meant to say it. The words just came out.

Silence on the other end of the line. Then: "She kept journals?"

"Handwritten. Years of them. They were in a box labeled KITCHEN."

More silence. Diane could hear Rebecca breathing, could hear the particular quality of that breathing that meant Rebecca was calculating something, was figuring out how this information fit into the larger picture she was always constructing.

"That could be useful," Rebecca said finally. "For the case. If there's anything about the platform, anything about what she knew—"

"I haven't finished reading them."

"But when you do—"

"When I do, I'll decide what to do."

"Mom." Rebecca's voice had an edge now. "These journals could be evidence. They could be the difference between—"

"Between what? Between winning and losing? You're going to win anyway. You have the emails, you have the spreadsheets, you have Alex's testimony. You don't need Zoe's private thoughts."

"Every piece of evidence matters. You know that. The more we can show that she knew what was happening, that she understood the system and was trapped in it anyway—"

"I'm not giving you her journals so you can use them to prove she was a victim."

"She WAS a victim."

"She was also a person. She was also my daughter. She was also—" Diane stopped. She didn't know how to finish the sentence. She was also funny, she wanted to say. She was also smart, and observant, and she noticed things about herself that I never noticed, and she wrote them down in a journal that nobody was supposed to read, and now I'm reading it, and I feel like I'm meeting her for the first time, which is absurd, because I raised her, I changed her diapers, I taught her to ride a bike, I was there for her first word and her first step and her first day of school, and yet somehow I never knew her, not really, not the her that she was when no one was watching.

"Mom?" Rebecca's voice was softer now. "Are you okay?"

"No," Diane said. "I'm not okay. But I'm going to keep reading."

She hung up before Rebecca could say anything else.

---

The journals covered nearly nine years. Diane didn't read them in order—she skipped around, following her instincts, stopping when something caught her eye.

2020, during the pandemic:

*I have not left this apartment in thirty-four days. I have posted content every day. My engagement has never been higher.*

*The world is falling apart and I am thriving. This probably says something about me, and it's probably not something good.*

*Mom called today. She asked if I was eating enough. I said yes. She asked if I was sleeping enough. I said yes. She asked if I needed anything. I said no. This is the same conversation we've had every week for ten years. I don't know why she keeps asking and I don't know why I keep answering. Neither of us says anything real. Neither of us asks the questions we actually want to ask.*

*What would I ask her, if I could ask her anything?*

*I would ask: Are you happy? Did you get the life you wanted? Do you ever think about the person you were before you became a mother, and do you miss her?*

*I would ask: Do you see me? Not the content. Me.*

*But I won't ask. And she won't ask me. And we'll keep having the same conversation, every week, until one of us is dead.*

Diane read this entry three times. Her eyes were blurring. She wasn't crying—she had done most of her crying in the first two months, had cried until crying started to feel performative, until she was watching herself cry and thinking "is this what grief looks like? Am I doing it right?"—but her eyes were blurring anyway, from tiredness maybe, from the strain of reading handwriting in bad light.

She turned on a lamp. One of Zoe's lamps. A ring light, actually, the kind Zoe used for filming. It was too bright, too white, the kind of light that made everything look exposed and harsh. But it was the only lamp within reach, and Diane didn't want to get up, didn't want to move from this spot on the floor where she had been sitting for—she checked her phone—eight hours.

She should eat something. She should go back to the hotel. She should sleep.

She kept reading.

---

2021, after the vaccines, after the world started opening up again:

*Berlin. I'm in Berlin. I came here because everyone said Berlin was the place to be if you were young and creative and wanted to "find yourself," which is a phrase I hate but also a thing I'm trying to do.*

*Met a boy. His name is Marcus. He's not famous. He doesn't have followers. He looked at me, the first time we talked, like I was a person and not a brand.*

*That sounds dramatic. It's not dramatic. It's just rare.*

*He said: "If no one's watching, did it even happen?"*

*I said: "We're watching each other."*

*He said: "Are we?"*

*I didn't have an answer. I still don't have an answer. But I liked that he asked.*

Diane knew about Marcus. Rebecca had interviewed him. He was part of the lawsuit—not as a defendant, but as a witness, someone who had been there, who had seen things. Rebecca said he was writing a book. "Everyone's writing a book," Rebecca had said, in a tone that made clear what she thought about everyone writing a book.

But here, in Zoe's journal, Marcus was just a boy she met in Berlin. A boy who asked a question she couldn't answer.

There was more about Marcus. Pages of it.

*March 2021*

*Marcus asked me today why I do this. "This" meaning the content, the posting, the whole performance.*

*I said: "Because I'm good at it."*

*He said: "That's not a reason. That's just a fact."*

*And I didn't have another answer. I didn't have a real reason. I do it because I started doing it and then I couldn't stop. I do it because stopping would mean admitting that everything I've built is—what? A mistake? A trap? A way of avoiding something I'm afraid to face?*

*What am I afraid to face?*

*I don't know. That's the honest answer. I don't know what I'm running from. I just know that I'm running, and the running feels necessary, and stopping feels impossible.*

*Marcus said: "You could just... not post. For a day. See what happens."*

*I laughed. I laughed like he had suggested I stop breathing.*

*He looked at me for a long time. Then he said: "That's the problem, isn't it?"*

*Yes. That's the problem. The posting has become breathing. The content has become survival. And I don't know how to undo that. I don't know if I want to undo that.*

*Maybe this is just what life is now. Maybe this is what it means to be a person in 2021. Maybe I'm not sick—maybe I'm just adapted. Maybe the people who aren't posting are the ones who are failing to adapt.*

*I don't believe that. But I tell myself I believe it, because the alternative is admitting that I've built my whole life around something that's killing me.*

---

*April 2021*

*Marcus left. Not dramatically—he just went back to Berlin, back to his job, back to his life that doesn't involve cameras and content and the endless performance of selfhood.*

*I could have gone with him. He asked me to. He said: "Come to Berlin. We'll get an apartment. You can figure out what you want to do."*

*And I thought about it. For about thirty seconds, I thought about it. A life without the platform. A life where I wake up and don't immediately check my metrics. A life where I exist without an audience.*

*It sounded peaceful.*

*It sounded like death.*

*I told him no. I told him I had commitments, obligations, a career. All of which was true. But the real reason—the reason I wrote in this journal because I couldn't say it out loud—was that I was afraid. I was afraid that without the platform, without the audience, I would discover that there was nothing underneath. That the performance was all there was. That the Zoe who existed for followers was the only Zoe, and the Zoe who existed without them was just... gone.*

*Marcus said: "I don't think that's true."*

*I said: "You don't know that."*

*He said: "Neither do you."*

*He's right. I don't know. And I'm not willing to find out. And that's the saddest thing I've written in this journal so far.*

---

Diane remembered April 2021.

She remembered it because Zoe had called her, uncharacteristically, in the middle of the day. Diane had been at work—she worked at a nonprofit that helped first-generation college students with applications, a job she had taken after the divorce because she needed something to do, something that felt meaningful, something that wasn't just waiting for her daughters to need her—and she had stepped outside to take the call, standing in the parking lot of the office building with the sun beating down on her head.

"Mom," Zoe had said. "What do you do when you're afraid of something but you don't know what it is?"

Diane had been surprised. Zoe didn't ask questions like that. Zoe didn't admit to being afraid of anything.

"I don't know," Diane had said, honestly. "I think... I think you try to figure out what it is. You try to name it."

"What if you can't name it? What if it's just this... feeling? This sense that something is wrong but you don't know what?"

Diane had wanted to say: yes, I know that feeling. I've felt it my whole life. It's the feeling of being a person who is supposed to be happy but isn't, who is supposed to be grateful but isn't, who has everything she's supposed to want and yet wants something else, something she can't name.

But she hadn't said that. She had said: "Maybe you should talk to someone. A therapist."

Zoe had laughed. "Right. Because that's what you do."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means you've never talked to a therapist in your life. It means you deal with everything by not dealing with it. It means—" She had stopped. "Sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to—"

"It's okay," Diane had said, even though it wasn't okay, even though Zoe's words had landed like a slap. "You're right. I'm not very good at talking about things."

"Neither am I," Zoe had said. "Maybe that's genetic."

They had both laughed, a little. An uncomfortable laugh, the kind that acknowledges a truth without actually dealing with it.

"I love you," Diane had said.

"I love you too," Zoe had said.

And then they had hung up, and Diane had gone back to work, and she had never mentioned the conversation again, and neither had Zoe, and now Diane was reading her daughter's journal and understanding, finally, what that conversation had been about.

Marcus. The fear. The sense that something was wrong but she couldn't name it.

Diane could name it now. Too late, but she could name it.

*June 2021*

*Something I've been thinking about: Mom and Dad got divorced when I was ten. I remember the day Dad moved out. I remember Mom standing in the kitchen looking out the window, not crying, just... still. Like someone had unplugged her.*

*I asked her: "Are you okay?"*

*She said: "I'm fine."*

*I knew she wasn't fine. Even at ten, I knew. But she said she was fine, and I accepted it, because what else could I do? I was ten. I didn't know how to push. I didn't know that adults sometimes need you to push.*

*I think about that a lot. The way we teach each other not to ask questions. The way we train each other to accept "I'm fine" as an answer even when it's obviously a lie.*

*Mom never talked about the divorce. Not really. She said things like "your father and I decided it was best" and "sometimes people grow apart." She never said: "I was unhappy. I was unhappy for years. I stayed because I was scared to leave, and then I left because I was more scared to stay."*

*But that's what happened. I know that's what happened. I've pieced it together from hints, from silences, from the things she didn't say.*

*Why didn't she say them? Why didn't she tell me the truth?*

*I know why. Because she was protecting me. Because she thought I was too young. Because she thought the truth would hurt me.*

*But the silence hurt me too. The silence taught me that there are things you don't talk about, feelings you don't acknowledge, truths you keep hidden even from the people you love.*

*And now I'm doing the same thing. I'm keeping truths hidden. I'm saying "I'm fine" when I'm not fine. I'm teaching the people around me not to ask questions.*

*Mom: if you ever read this, I want you to know that I understand. I understand why you didn't tell me the truth about the divorce. I understand why you protected me.*

*But I also wish you hadn't. I wish you had told me: "I was unhappy, and leaving was hard, and I don't have all the answers." I wish you had shown me that it was okay to be confused, to be scared, to not know what you're doing.*

*Instead, you showed me how to be silent. And I learned that lesson well.*

---

Diane had to put the journal down.

She walked to the bathroom. She turned on the faucet and let the water run for a long time, cold, over her hands. She looked at herself in the mirror—the face of a woman who was fifty-eight and tired and had just read her dead daughter's assessment of her parenting—and she thought: she's right.

She was right about all of it.

Diane had been silent. Diane had said "I'm fine" when she wasn't fine. Diane had protected her daughters from the truth of the divorce and in doing so had taught them that the truth was something to be hidden.

And Zoe had learned that lesson. Had learned it so well that she had hidden her own truths, even the ones that were killing her, even the ones that might have saved her life if she had spoken them out loud.

Was that Diane's fault?

Dr. Okonkwo would say no. Dr. Okonkwo would say that parents do the best they can, that children make their own choices, that the chain of cause and effect is not so simple.

But Diane, standing in her dead daughter's bathroom with the water running cold over her hands, thought: maybe it's not about fault. Maybe it's not about blame. Maybe it's just about understanding—understanding the ways we shape each other, the ways our silences echo, the ways the things we don't say become the things our children don't say.

She turned off the water.

She went back to the journals.

---

2022, London:

*Alex. The manager. The one Flux assigned to me.*

*They're smart. They're very smart. They understand the algorithm in a way that I'm only starting to understand it. They look at my analytics and they see patterns I can't see. They tell me what to post, when to post, how to post. They tell me that vulnerability performs.*

*"Vulnerability performs." That's a thing Alex said. They said it like it was obvious, like everyone knew this, like I should have known this already.*

*And I did know it. I just hadn't said it out loud. I hadn't put it in words.*

*Vulnerability performs. Crisis performs. The worse I feel, the better my content does. The worse my content makes me feel, the better it performs.*

*This is a loop. This is a trap. This is a machine, and I am inside it, and I don't know how to get out, and I'm not sure I want to get out, because outside the machine is what? Obscurity. Invisibility. The terror of being nobody.*

*I told Alex this. I said: "I think the algorithm is optimizing for my destruction."*

*Alex looked at me for a long time. Then they said: "The algorithm doesn't have intentions. It just finds patterns."*

*This is either reassuring or terrifying. I think it's supposed to be reassuring. I think Alex thinks they're reassuring me. But what I heard was: "There's no one driving the car. The car is driving itself. And it's driving toward a cliff. But don't worry—there's no one to blame."*

---

Diane put the journal down.

She stood up. Her knees protested—she had been sitting on the floor for too long, in the way of someone who was fifty-eight and didn't move the way she used to. She walked to the window.

The apartment was on the fourteenth floor. Below, Manhattan continued in its Manhattan way—people walking, cars moving, lights blinking, the whole indifferent organism of the city doing what it did. Zoe had lived here for the last year of her life. She had looked out this window. She had seen this view.

What had she thought about, looking at it?

Diane didn't know. She would never know. This was the cruelty of death—not just that the person was gone, but that all the things you would have learned about them, all the conversations you would have had, all the small moments of understanding that accumulate over a life and turn strangers into intimates, all of that was gone too. Death took not just the person but the future you would have had with them, all the versions of them you would have met as they changed and grew and became new people, which is what people do, which is what life is, a continuous process of becoming someone slightly different from who you were before, and death stops that, death freezes you at the last version of yourself, and everyone who loved you is stuck with that version forever, unable to see what you might have become.

Zoe would have become someone.

Zoe would have changed, would have maybe gotten off the platform, would have maybe found a different way to live, would have maybe—

But no. That was wishful thinking. That was the kind of story survivors told themselves to make the loss bearable. Diane didn't know what Zoe would have become. Maybe she would have gotten worse. Maybe she would have died anyway, in a different way, at a different time. Maybe the trajectory was set and nothing could have changed it.

The grief counselor said this kind of thinking wasn't helpful. "You can't know what would have happened," Dr. Okonkwo had said. "You can only know what did happen. And what happened is painful enough without adding hypotheticals."

But Diane couldn't stop adding hypotheticals. Her brain did it automatically, constructing alternate timelines in which Zoe was alive, in which Diane had said something different, done something different, been a different kind of mother.

---

She found the entry about camp. The camp in Vermont, the camp without screens, the camp Zoe had hated.

*I've been thinking about camp. The one Mom sent me to when I was eleven. The one where I cried every night and called her begging to come home.*

*I hated it at the time. I hated the woods and the bugs and the other kids and the counselors with their forced enthusiasm. Most of all I hated being away from my computer, away from the internet, away from the world I had built online where I had friends, sort of, people who knew me, sort of, people who saw me.*

*But here's the thing: that week at camp, the week before Mom finally let me come home early, is the last time I remember being bored.*

*Not bored in a bad way. Bored in the way where your brain has nothing to do, so it starts doing things on its own. I remember lying on my bunk looking at the ceiling and thinking about nothing in particular, letting my thoughts wander wherever they wanted to go. I remember the feeling of time passing slowly, each hour a whole country to be explored.*

*I don't feel that anymore. I can't remember the last time I felt that. Now every moment is filled—with content, with consumption, with the endless scroll. Even when I'm not looking at my phone, I'm thinking about what I'll post later, composing captions in my head, narrating my experience for an audience that isn't there.*

*Mom was right about the camp. She was right and I hated her for it, and then I forgot about it, and now I'm remembering it, and I wish I could tell her she was right.*

*I won't tell her. It would require explaining too much. It would require admitting that I'm not okay, that I haven't been okay for a long time, that the life I've built is a trap I walked into willingly and now I can't find the door.*

*But I'm writing it here. That's something. That's mine.*

---

Diane had to stop reading.

She went to the kitchen—the kitchen that didn't have a real stove, the kitchen where Zoe had probably eaten takeout every night, had probably never cooked a meal, had probably never needed to because when you made the kind of money Zoe made you could just order whatever you wanted whenever you wanted it, the world came to you, you didn't have to go to the world—and opened the refrigerator.

It was empty. Of course it was empty. Rebecca had cleaned it out weeks ago, had thrown away whatever food was left, had scrubbed the shelves with baking soda and left the door propped open so it wouldn't smell.

Diane closed the refrigerator. She opened a cabinet. Inside were protein bars, dozens of them, all the same brand, something called "Optimal Fuel" that promised "sustained energy for peak performance."

She took one. She opened it. She ate it standing in the kitchen of her dead daughter's apartment.

It tasted like cardboard and chemicals. It was probably exactly what Zoe had eaten for breakfast, lunch, and dinner. It was fuel, not food. It was optimized, not enjoyed.

Diane threw the rest of it away.

---

The last section of the journal—the entries from 2027, from the final year—were harder to read. The handwriting was less steady. There were gaps, sometimes weeks between entries. And the tone had changed.

*September 3, 2027*

*I know about the program.*

*I wasn't supposed to know. No one told me. But you can't spend this much time studying algorithms without starting to understand when an algorithm is studying you back.*

*They call it Cassandra. After the prophet no one believed. Funny, right? Or not funny. One of those.*

*It predicts which creators are going to die. It predicts the timeline. And then—this is the part I'm still wrapping my head around—it optimizes for engagement during the decline. It promotes the content that shows the spiral. It rewards the posts about crisis and pain and not sleeping and not eating and wanting to disappear.*

*The algorithm wants me to die. Not because it's evil. Because dead creators generate engagement. Because the content of dying is the best content there is.*

*I should be scared. I should be angry. I should be something.*

*I'm not anything. I'm just tired.*

---

*October 15, 2027*

*Thought about telling Mom. Didn't tell Mom.*

*What would I say? "Hey Mom, the platform I work for has a program that predicts I'm going to die, and they're making money off it, and I know this and I'm still posting, because posting is the only thing I know how to do, because without the posting I'm nobody, because the alternative to being content is being nothing."*

*She wouldn't understand. She would try to understand, she would nod and say "mm-hmm" and make that face she makes when she's worried, but she wouldn't understand, because her generation didn't grow up with this, her generation still thinks the internet is a place you visit rather than a place you live.*

*But also: she would understand. She would understand the part about being nobody. She would understand the fear of disappearing. Because I've seen it in her—the way she defers to other people, the way she makes herself small, the way she moves through the world like she's apologizing for taking up space.*

*We're the same. We're both afraid of disappearing. We just have different ways of trying to stay visible.*

---

*November 12, 2027*

*Sixteen days.*

*That's what I have, according to Cassandra. Sixteen days. Give or take.*

*I don't know if I believe it. I don't know if it's accurate. The model could be wrong. I could be an outlier.*

*But I also think: what if I'm not? What if sixteen days is exactly right? What would I do differently?*

*The answer, I'm realizing, is nothing. I would do nothing differently. I would keep posting. I would keep performing. I would keep being content until I stopped being anything.*

*This is probably something I should tell someone. A therapist. My sister. My mom.*

*I'm not going to tell anyone. I'm going to write it here, in this journal that no one will read until I'm dead. And then someone—Becca, probably, because Becca is the one who goes through things, who looks for evidence, who turns everything into a case—will read this and think: she knew. She knew and she didn't stop.*

*And they'll be right. I know and I'm not stopping. Because stopping is disappearing. Because stopping is becoming nobody. Because I would rather—*

*I started to write "I would rather die than disappear" and then I stopped because I wasn't sure if it was true.*

*Now I'm pretty sure it's true.*

*That's probably something I should tell someone.*

*I'm telling you. Whoever you are, reading this. I'm telling you: I knew. I chose. The machine gave me a choice—visibility or life—and I chose visibility. I don't expect you to understand. I don't even expect you to forgive me.*

*I just wanted someone to know that it was a choice. That I wasn't just a victim. That I saw what was happening and I let it happen because the alternative was worse.*

*Is that better? Is it better to be a person who chose their destruction than a person who stumbled into it?*

*I don't know. I'll never know. In sixteen days (give or take), I won't know anything.*

*This is the only thing I won't post. This is mine.*

---

*November 25, 2027*

*Three days.*

*I'm writing this because I need to say something that I can't post. I need to say it somewhere that no one will see.*

*Becca: I'm sorry. I know you're going to find this. I know you're going to turn it into evidence. I know you're going to build a case, because that's what you do when you can't just feel things. I'm not judging. I do the same thing. I turn everything into content because I can't just feel things either. We're the same. We just have different platforms.*

*Mom: I'm sorry I didn't call more. I'm sorry I didn't come home for Christmas last year. I'm sorry I made everything about me, even when you were going through things, even when you needed me. I was selfish. I knew I was being selfish. I told myself I would make it up to you later.*

*There is no later. I know that now.*

*I love you both. I should have said it more. I should have said it in ways that weren't content.*

*If you're reading this, you're probably wondering: why didn't you ask for help? Why didn't you tell someone? Why didn't you fight?*

*I don't have a good answer. I don't have any answer. I'm just tired. I've been tired for a long time. And the algorithm is very good at keeping you running even when you're tired, even when you want to stop, because stopping means disappearing, and disappearing is the worst thing, the thing that I have been taught since I was eleven years old is the worst thing.*

*Remember camp? Remember how I cried every night and begged you to let me come home?*

*You were right, Mom. You were right about the camp. You were right that I needed to be bored, that I needed to be disconnected, that I needed to learn how to exist without an audience.*

*I didn't learn. I'm sorry. I wish I had.*

*This is the only thing I won't post. This is mine.*

---

Diane finished reading.

She sat on the floor of the apartment for a long time. The ring light was still on, casting its harsh white glow over everything.

Outside, Manhattan continued. The city didn't care about her grief. The city had seen countless people grieve, had absorbed their losses into its endless appetite for more, more people, more stories, more content.

Content. There was that word again.

Diane stood up. Her whole body ached. She walked to the box labeled KITCHEN and looked at the journals—nine years of her daughter's private thoughts, the only part of Zoe that had never been shared, that had never been optimized, that had never been content.

She thought about Rebecca, who was waiting for evidence. She thought about the lawsuit, which would succeed or fail regardless of what Diane did with these journals. She thought about what Zoe had written: *I know you're going to turn it into evidence.*

She thought about her own journal, the one she had thrown away during the divorce. She thought about how much she wished she could read it now, could remember who she had been, could have that conversation with her past self that she would never be able to have with her dead daughter.

She picked up the journals.

She carried them to the kitchen.

She stood in front of the sink for a long time.

---

The grief counselor had said that grief was not a problem to be solved. "It's not something you get over," Dr. Okonkwo had said, with the water feature making its irritating sound in the background. "It's something you learn to carry. Some days it will be heavy. Some days it will be lighter. But it will always be with you."

Diane had nodded and said "mm-hmm" and thought: what does that mean? What does it mean to carry something forever? How do you carry something that weighs more than you do?

Now, standing in her dead daughter's kitchen with a stack of journals in her hands, she thought she understood.

You carry it the way you carry anything. One day at a time. One moment at a time. You put it down sometimes, when you need to rest. You pick it back up when you're ready.

And sometimes—sometimes—you make a choice about what to do with it. You decide whether to keep it private or make it public. Whether to let it become evidence or let it remain what it was always meant to be: something that belongs to the person who wrote it, something that no one else has a right to see, something that is theirs.

*This is the only thing I won't post. This is mine.*

Diane put the journals back in the box.

She closed the box.

She picked up her phone and called Rebecca.

"Mom?" Rebecca's voice was cautious. "Are you okay?"

"I finished reading the journals."

Silence. Diane could hear Rebecca holding her breath.

"They're not evidence," Diane said. "They're just... her. The part of her that she kept private. The part that wasn't content."

"Mom—"

"I'm not giving them to you."

More silence. Then: "What are you going to do with them?"

Diane looked at the box. She thought about the choices you make, the things you keep and the things you throw away. She thought about her own journal, lost forever, and her daughter's journals, here in her hands.

"I'm going to read them again," she said. "And then I'm going to keep them. They're hers. They're mine now. That's what she wanted."

"She wanted them to stay private? She said that?"

"She said: this is the only thing I won't post. This is mine."

Rebecca was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice was softer.

"Okay," she said. "Okay, Mom. If that's what you want."

"It's what she wanted."

"Then it's what she wanted."

They stayed on the phone for a while longer, not saying much. Just breathing together. Just being together, the way you are with family, even when you have nothing left to say.

---

Later—much later, after Diane had turned off the ring light and locked the apartment and gone back to her hotel room with the box of journals in her arms—she thought about what Zoe had written about camp.

*Mom was right about the camp. She was right and I hated her for it.*

Diane had been right. Once. About one thing. It wasn't much, but it was something.

She opened the first journal again. She started reading from the beginning.

*I'm starting this because I need somewhere to put the parts of me that aren't content.*

*This is mine.*

Diane read until she fell asleep, and when she woke up, she kept reading, and when she finished, she started again, learning her daughter, finally, too late, in the only way she could now.

The only way that was left.
